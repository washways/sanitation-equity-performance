Better sanitation is not only about better toilets.
It is also about who can afford them.

This tool asks a simple question: which sanitation choices reach poorer households and keep the most faecal waste out of the environment?

Imagine 1,000 households. Each household has different resources and makes a discrete yes/no sanitation decision under the scenario. A higher-performing toilet may control more waste for each household, but if it is too expensive, many households may be left without one.

Using household-level stochastic microsimulation matters because real households do not adopt “37% of a toilet”. Coverage emerges from 1,000 individual decisions, so affordability thresholds, inequality and targeting can produce nonlinear community outcomes.

Method

The model is a structural scenario, not a forecast of future sanitation coverage or household behaviour. It is a reproducible stochastic microsimulation of 1,000 households representing financial access, discrete technology choice and technical faecal-load performance. Existing infrastructure, supply chains, land and tenure constraints, water availability, social preferences, regulation and programme delivery are not predicted. The full synthetic household-level dataset can be opened from the map.

1

Represent inequality

GNI per capita sets the scale. Gini shapes a stylised household-resource distribution.

2

Estimate financial access to sanitation

cash budget = annual household resources × selected upfront resource share
financial access = logistic(2.0 × ln(cash budget ÷ effective technology price))

GNI per capita and Gini create a stylised monetary resource distribution. The default assumes 5% of annual resources can be mobilised upfront for sanitation, but this is explicitly editable. A simple soft transition around the price threshold avoids the unrealistic assumption that two otherwise similar households switch instantly from 0% to 100% access. These are transparent economic scenario assumptions. JMP inequality data are not used to fit them.

JMP wealth-quintile observations are kept outside the model and shown separately as an independent reality check on whether the resulting equity pattern resembles observed sanitation inequality.

3

Turn probabilities into household decisions

household adopts an option if financial-access probability ≥ its fixed random threshold

The model uses 1,000 synthetic households rather than applying one average adoption percentage to the whole population. Each household receives a reproducible pseudo-random threshold and therefore makes a discrete yes/no decision for each scenario. This means community coverage, technology mix and subsidy requirements emerge from individual household outcomes.

This stochastic microsimulation is useful around affordability thresholds: two households with similar resources need not always make the same realised choice, and small price or subsidy changes can shift some households but not others. The random thresholds are fixed between runs so comparisons are reproducible. They represent unobserved heterogeneity, not a measured behavioural model or a confidence interval.

4

Separate access from technical performance

A: any household toilet stops open defecation
B: faecal-load performance varies by technology

Cost is not used as a proxy for technical performance.

5

Estimate faecal load kept out of the environment

annual wet faecal mass = population × 128 g/person/day × 365
load kept out = Σ household faecal mass × technology performance

In Strategy Lab, the technical-performance sensitivity band applies a common plus/minus percentage-point range around the central technology assumptions while holding household choices fixed. It is a sensitivity test, not a confidence interval.

The 128 g/person/day conversion is the global median wet faecal mass reported by Rose et al. (2015). It is used only to translate the scenario into an intuitive annual mass. The technology performance percentages remain editable assumptions and are not pathogen-risk estimates.

The Strategy lab is intentionally simple. It tests only household support, market price/technical-performance assumptions and take-up. Market strengthening, local production or R&D can be explored by changing technology prices directly rather than imposing a speculative relationship between programme spending and the resulting price reduction.

6

Shuffle households across the village

Wealth rank is assigned to building footprints through a fixed random permutation. When affordability changes, colours change across the village rather than from one side to the other.

Evidence

The evidence supports the structure of the model, not one universal cost-performance curve. WHO emphasizes safety across the whole sanitation service chain, while affordability, subsidy design and financing affect who can obtain and use sanitation. The global comparison page adds an external plausibility check against WHO/UNICEF JMP 2024 sanitation estimates.

Price affects uptake

Randomised sanitation subsidies in Bangladesh increased hygienic latrine ownership and reduced open defecation.

Science 2015

Liquidity matters

Finance substantially increased willingness to purchase sanitation in Cambodia.

BenYishay et al.

Coverage affects contamination

Higher local latrine coverage in Bangladesh was associated with lower E. coli contamination.

Contreras et al.

Containment matters

Environmental contamination in Accra differed sharply around clusters of contained sanitation.

Berendes et al.

Averages can hide exclusion

WHO and UNICEF recommend affordability analysis across poorer households, not only national averages.

WHO / UNICEF 2021

Safety is a service-chain outcome

WHO defines safe sanitation across toilet capture, containment, emptying, conveyance, treatment and final disposal or reuse. Capital cost alone is therefore not a measure of environmental performance.

WHO sanitation guidelines

Subsidy design affects equity

World Bank evidence shows poorly designed water and sanitation subsidies often benefit richer households disproportionately, while targeted approaches can improve equity.

World Bank 2019

Data and interpretation

Sanitation costs. Country unit costs come from Hutton & Varughese's World Bank sanitation costing study, which compiled country cost evidence and extrapolated costs for 140 low- and middle-income countries.

Country scope. The analysis covers the current UN Least Developed Countries, where household financial access remains especially relevant to sanitation access.

Financial-access model. Published sanitation capital costs are combined with GNI per capita and Gini to create a stylised monetary resource distribution. The affordability share and soft transition are scenario assumptions. JMP inequality data do not affect the model calculation.

Cost mapping used

  • Traditional pit: World Bank 2015 rural unimproved-pit capital cost per person.
  • Improved pit: mean of the published rural wet-pit and dry-pit capital costs, used as a broad basic-improved-pit proxy.
  • Septic: published latrine-with-septic-tank cost, shown in the table for context but not treated as equivalent to safely managed sanitation.
  • 2015 US-dollar costs are converted to 2024 dollars using the US CPI ratio 143.86 / 108.7 = 1.323.

Remaining limitations

  • Published unit costs are estimates, and some source data are old. They should be treated as indicative country benchmarks rather than current market quotations.
  • GNI per capita and Gini are used to reconstruct a stylised resource distribution. Missing Gini values use the median of the LDCs with data and are flagged.
  • The affordability calculation is expressed per person, avoiding the need to assume one household size across all LDCs.
  • Financial access is not the only determinant of sanitation uptake. Supply chains, tenure, norms, public investment, urbanisation and policy matter.
  • Correlation with JMP does not establish causation. Income and sanitation access share many underlying determinants.

National comparison sources. LDC membership: UN Statistics Division M49. Sanitation unit costs: World Bank, Hutton & Varughese (2016), Appendix E. GNI/Gini and national JMP 2024 sanitation indicators: World Development Indicators.

Independent JMP inequality benchmark. Country inequality workbooks from WHO/UNICEF JMP provide observed sanitation outcomes by survey wealth quintile from re-analysed household surveys such as DHS and MICS. They do not determine the model's resource distribution, affordability share, technology costs or technical performance. They are shown alongside the economic model as an independent reality check. The embedded benchmark contains 380 observed quintile outcomes. Six basic-sanitation series were reconstructed from the mutually exclusive JMP sanitation ladder after the initial extraction selected an inconsistent row; Comoros was excluded because its extracted ladder remained internally inconsistent.

Compare the economic scenario with observed JMP inequality data

JMP inequality data have one specific role here: they provide an independent observed Q1–Q5 benchmark. They do not set the model's household resources, affordability assumptions, technology shares, sanitation costs, technical performance or faecal-load assumptions.

How JMP inequality data are used in this tool
1 · Build the economic scenarioGNI + Gini create a synthetic monetary resource distribution. Sanitation costs then determine modelled financial access.
2 · Keep JMP outside the modelJMP Q1–Q5 observations are not used to fit household resources, the 5% budget assumption, technology choice or technical performance.
3 · Compare with observed inequalityFor the benchmark only, income, Gini and costs are aligned to the JMP survey period and the model is compared with the observed Q1–Q5 sanitation pattern.
4 · Learn from the gapIf modelled financial access differs from observed sanitation, that highlights the importance of supply, tenure, behaviour, public investment and other non-price factors.

What this analysis tests

The analysis tests whether differences in sanitation costs, household resources and inequality are associated with observed JMP sanitation access across LDCs. The embedded wealth-quintile validation separately tests whether the simple financial-access model reproduces observed equity gradients. Neither comparison is treated as a forecast.
